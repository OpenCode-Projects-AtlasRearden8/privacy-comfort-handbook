# Browser and phone privacy settings

*Reading time: about 10 minutes.*

This is the chapter where a lot of privacy guides lose people in forty menu screenshots. Not this one. Here is the honest secret of settings: most of the protection comes from a handful of choices, and the rest belongs to people who enjoy menus. This is the handful.

Plan for a twenty-minute tidy, tea included. Do the browser first, then the phone.

## Browsers: five things that matter

1. **Update first.** A browser's most important privacy feature is being new. Old browsers are the open window that trackers and scams alike prod for cracks. If updates happen automatically, you have already done this.
2. **Block third-party cookies.** First-party cookies are the site's own memory of you — the shopping cart, the logged-in session. Third-party cookies are notes left by everyone *else* embedded in the page: the ad networks and trackers from chapter 01. Blocking the second kind costs you almost nothing in daily life. Every major browser has a "Privacy" or "Security" section with a settings page containing a "third-party cookies" switch; set it to block.

   A small aside: cookie banners are, in some countries, required to offer a real "reject" button, and many do. When in doubt: "Reject all" is a fully respectable answer, and nowhere near as dramatic as it sounds.
3. **Know what a private window really does.** Private or incognito mode deletes your history *on that device only* when you close it. It hides your browsing from a roommate or a shared family computer. It does not hide you from the websites themselves — they still see your visit exactly as always. It is a guest room, not a disguise; both truths are worth knowing if you use it for anything you care about.
4. **Keep extensions few and boring.** Extensions can see almost everything you browse — they are little houseguests with house keys. The good pattern: two or three, from the official store, each earning its place. Dead giveaway of a shady one: it asks for "read and change all data on all sites" and is not one you chose deliberately. When in doubt, remove it; you can add it back in ten seconds.
5. **Choose your search engine like choosing a doctor.** Fine either way: big search engines index everything and personalize; search engines like DuckDuckGo or Startpage trade some comfort for less tracking. Neither is wrong. "Search engine" is near the top of your browser's settings page, and it is a two-minute swap.

Extensions confuse new people the most, so one friendly example of the good pattern: a password manager extension (from chapter 03) plus optionally an ad blocker. That is a complete, defensible browser setup for almost everyone. Everything else is decoration.

## Phones: the permission audit

The single most effective phone habit is the **permission audit**, and it takes ten unhurried minutes:

1. Open your phone's Settings app and find the "Privacy" or "Permissions" area (phones vary — "Privacy" is a reliable search term inside Settings).
2. Go category by category — location, microphone, camera, contacts — and read who has what.
3. Apply the three-level rule:
   - **"While using the app"** for anything that plausibly needs it: maps while driving.
   - **"Ask every time"** (your phone offers this!) for maybe-once-a-month things: a restaurant app and the camera.
   - **"Never"** for the suspicious: a flashlight with your location history is not a flashlight, it is a location logger with a light on top.

Two more small phone things, both fast:

- **The little dots are your friends.** The green/orange dot that appears by your camera or microphone indicator means "*recording now*." If it appears while an app is merely open, that app loses mic access — the audit above will find it.
- **Turn OFF reset and diagnostic sharing** if your phone offers "reset advertising ID" and "limit ad tracking" toggles — mostly they make ads dumber rather than protecting you. Use them anyway; dumb ads are quiet ads.

## The "do I need a VPN?" question, honestly

You will be advertised VPNs relentlessly, so here is the friend's version:

- **A VPN is genuinely useful** on hotel, airport, or café Wi-Fi, where you don't control the network and don't fully trust it. It is also useful if you specifically want your internet provider to know less about which sites you visit.
- **A VPN does not make you anonymous.** The VPN company can see nearly everything your provider used to see — trust merely moves. It does not stop phishing, account break-ins, or data collection by logged-in apps.
- So: **a small reputable VPN for travel Wi-Fi is a sensible purchase.** "A VPN because I saw an ad" is not. Free VPNs deserve extra suspicion: the business model of a VPN with no customers is the classic head-scratcher.

## The twenty-minute tidy, assembled

If you like having the whole routine in one place:

1. Browser: update, block third-party cookies, prune extensions. *(about 6 minutes)*
2. Phone: run the permission audit, category by category. *(about 10 minutes)*
3. Phone: glance at "camera/mic in use" dots for one ordinary day, as an optional game. *(the rest of the day, hands-free)*
4. Smile knowingly at the next VPN ad. *(0 minutes)*

And when a setting does not exist on your device, or the menu name differs — that is fine and normal. The *categories* (cookies, permissions, updates) are stable; the labels move yearly like furniture in a house you visit twice a year.

> **Remember:** settings are not a moral project and you are not behind. Twenty tidy minutes a year is a genuinely good privacy habit; nobody needs forty.
