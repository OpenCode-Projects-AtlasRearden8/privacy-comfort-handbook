# Glossary

*Plain-English definitions, ordered alphabetically. Each entry is one to three sentences — the way a friend would explain it, not the way a textbook would.*

## 2FA / two-factor authentication

A login plus a second proof that it is really you — usually a code from your phone or a tap on a device you own. Even if a thief learns your password, they still fail at this second door. Chapter 03 covers setting it up.

## Authenticator app

A phone app that shows rotating six-digit codes used for 2FA. The codes are generated on your phone rather than texted to you, which makes them harder to intercept and easy to use offline.

## Breach

An incident where a company loses control of its stored data — emails, passwords, order histories — and it ends up circulating outside the company. It says you were a customer; it does not say your devices were compromised. Chapter 07 is the drill.

## Browser

The app you use to visit websites: the program with the address bar in it. Chrome, Safari, Firefox, and Edge are the familiar ones. Cleaning up its settings once is Chapter 05's six-minute job.

## Cache

A browser's storage of recently downloaded pieces of websites, so pages load faster next time. Clearing it is harmless tidying — it only briefly makes sites load "cold."

## Cookie

A small note a website saves on your device so it can remember you: your cart, your language, your login. First-party cookies come from the site itself; third-party cookies come from other companies embedded in the page, and those are the ones worth blocking.

## Data broker

A company whose business is collecting information about people and selling it. You rarely meet one directly — you meet their outputs: oddly specific ads and people-search sites.

## Device fingerprint

The combination of small device traits — screen size, language, fonts, dozens more — that together can identify your device even without cookies. If a site blocks cookies, fingerprints are its next guess; that is why blocking cookies but not panicking is a reasonable stance.

## E2EE / end-to-end encryption

Scrambling a message on your phone so it only unscrambles on the recipient's, with the app company unable to read along. Chapter 01 shows what it does and — importantly — what it does not protect against (forwarding, screenshots, recipients).

## Email alias

A made-up extra address (or a variation, like adding `+shop` to your address) that forwards to your real inbox. Handy for keeping one site's mail untangled from another's — and for spotting who sold your address to a spammer.

## Encryption

Turning readable data into scrambled data that only someone with the right key can unscramble. It is the reason your password manager's contents are safe even if the file itself were stolen.

## HTTPS

The lock icon and padlock next to a website address: the traffic between you and that site is encrypted. Modern browsers enforce or warn about this so reliably that you mostly never think about it — which is how it should be.

## Incognito / private window

A browser window that forgets your history on that device when closed. A guest room, not a disguise: websites still see the visit (Chapter 05 says exactly where it stops working).

## IP address

The return address stamped on all of your device's internet traffic. It identifies your network — roughly a neighborhood, sometimes an exact home for small businesses — but not who you are as a person. Websites see it on every visit by design.

## Metadata

The envelope, not the letter: who you talked to, when, for how long, from where. Even chat apps with strong encryption still see metadata, which is why "who" and "how often" are worth knowing are collected.

## Passkey

A newer login method where your phone or computer proves it is you with your fingerprint, face, or device itself — no password to type and nothing for a phishing site to steal. If a site offers it, it is a genuinely good day.

## Passphrase

A password made of several unrelated words, like `maple-runway-71-brunch`. Long, strong, and actually memorable — the standing recommendation of Chapter 03.

## Password manager

An app that remembers all your different passwords behind one master password, like an address book where each entry is a login instead of a phone number. Chapter 03 covers the beginner setup.

## Permission

An app's granted ability to use a piece of your device: location, camera, microphone, contacts. Auditing these is Chapter 05's ten-minute phone habit.

## Phishing

A scam message pretending to be someone official — a bank, a parcel service, a colleague — to make you hand over money, codes, or logins. The seven red flags live in Chapter 04 and fit on an index card.

## Recovery codes

The one-time backup codes a website gives you when you turn on 2FA — the spare key for the day your phone is lost or replaced. Chapter 03 suggests the calm way to store them.

## SIM swap

A scam where a thief convinces a phone company to move your phone number to their SIM card, so calls and texts — including SMS codes — arrive for them. It is the reason app-generated codes beat texted ones on important accounts.

## Tracker

A small piece of code from a third party embedded in a page you visit, quietly telling that third party you stopped by. Share buttons, embeds, and ad pixels are common carriers — Chapter 01 introduces the family.

## VPN

A service that tunnels your internet traffic through another company's computers, hiding your browsing from the network you are on — while showing it, in full, to the VPN company instead. Genuinely useful for hotel Wi-Fi; not the anonymity switch its ads claim.
