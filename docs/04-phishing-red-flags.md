# Phishing red flags

*Reading time: about 9 minutes.*

Phishing — the word itself always sounded a little technical, so here is the plainer one: **a scam message pretending to be someone official.** An email, a text, a DM, a phone call. It could be a "bank," a "parcel service," an "IT team," a "grandchild."

Good news, and it is genuinely good: nearly every one of them runs on the same tiny script. You are about to learn the whole script. Seven flags fit on an index card, and together they catch almost everything.

## The seven red flags

1. **Manufactured urgency.** "Your account will be deleted in 24 hours." "Last warning." Act-now fear is the engine of every scam, because a running person cannot think. Slowness is the superpower here — real organizations give you days, and almost nothing legitimate is destroyed overnight.
2. **A mismatched sender address.** The name says "Microsoft Security," but tap the name and the real address is `security@mutms034-tickets.com`. Official email comes from the company's own domain, every time. One honest caveat: scammers can fake the *display name*, so this check is strong but best combined with the other six.
3. **A link that goes somewhere else.** Hover over it on a computer (or long-press on a phone) and read the address preview. `tracking-ups.com` going nowhere near ups.com means: do not click, and no need even to report it — just let it be deleted.
4. **An attachment you did not ask for.** "INVOICE_0921.pdf", "VoiceMessage1124.m4a", anything with a doubled file extension. If you did not request a document, treat it as a brick you were handed on the street: you wouldn't open it, either.
5. **Payment in strange currencies.** Real organizations accept real money. Gift cards, wire transfers to "secure accounts," cryptocurrency asks, "small verification fees" — all of that, from any organization, or from anything about a refund, is scam-shaped.
6. **Too good to be true.** The sweepstake you never entered, the refund earned without a claim, the inheritance, the bonus. Someone learned the one trick a fortune teller never tops: free money goes to people who did not apply for it.
7. **Asking for codes in sequence.** A password typed first, then a texted code "to confirm" — that is your 2FA code being relayed to a thief who is signing in as you, live. No legitimate process ever needs the password and the code at the same time from you.

## It helps to know what it sounds like

Scam messages copy a script. Here is a typical sample, annotated:

```text
"Dear valued customer — your Apple ID has been SUSPENDED.
Please sign in within 24 HOURS at hxxp://appleid-support-verify
You will receive gift card refund. Sincerely, APPLE SUPPORT TEAM."
```

Read it like a friend would read it to you:

- "Dear valued customer" — an organization that knows you uses your name; scammers don't, so they write to nobody in particular.
- "SUSPENDED ... 24 HOURS" — flags one, loud and proud.
- A mangled link — flag three, wearing a costume.
- "gift card refund" — flags five *and* six having dinner together.

Once you have read a handful like this, flag-spotting becomes oddly fun, like finding a costar in an old show.

## "A person from the bank called me": the phone version

The same flags work on calls. Two plain rules, nearly foolproof:

1. **Hang up and dial from your own resources.** The bank's real number is on the back of your card, printed on your statement — dial it yourself and ask.
2. **Screen-sharing is not required for verification.** No legitimate process ever needs to watch you type a password. If someone says "let's use screen sharing so I can log in for you," that sentence is the first and last red flag.

## If you clicked or typed: the calm response

One in ten of us will do this eventually; it is called phishing because it *works* on people who are busy, tired, or simply having a bad Tuesday. So — no shame. Just the drill:

1. **If you typed a password:** change it right now (chapter 03 style: strong, long, unique) and sign out all sessions on that account.
2. **If you typed a 2FA code or approved a "sign-in":** go to the account's security page, sign out all sessions, change the password, and check for anything quietly added — new forwarding rules, unknown devices, mystery third-party apps.
3. **If a payment went out:** call your bank immediately, dialing the number from the back of your card, and say the words "I was the victim of a scam." It is a completely ordinary phrase to hear at a bank; it gets you to the team that can freeze and sometimes reverse things.
4. **If you only clicked a link and typed nothing:** you are almost certainly fine. A quick look at the account's recent sign-ins, just for peace of mind, is plenty.
5. **Freeze what you can.** A credit freeze is free in many places (chapter 02 touches on it), and locks new-account fraud out.

One more kind thing to say: report when you have the energy, not out of fear. Many places have official — and conveniently named — channels. In the United States there is the FTC's site reportfraud.ftc.gov; in the United Kingdom there is actionfraud.police.uk; Australia has cyber.gov.au. These sites change names occasionally — if a link is dead, search the name. Reporting is worthwhile mainly because it quietly builds the mapping that filters tomorrow's spam for everyone.

## The one-sentence version for the fridge

Pause, then verify through a channel **you chose yourself.** No urgency, no link, no call-back number they gave you.

> **Remember:** getting fooled is a moment, not the kind of person you are. Smart, tired, distracted people fall for good scripts — and the fix is always the same: slow the moment down and call them.
