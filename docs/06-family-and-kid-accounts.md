# Family, kids, and accounts

*Reading time: about 10 minutes.*

Privacy planning gets more interesting — and honestly, more meaningful — the moment other people are involved. A household runs on a handful of shared devices, shared subscriptions, and a small cast of kids, teens, and grandparents, all of whom have very different needs.

Two ground rules for this whole chapter:

1. **Scaffolding, not surveillance.** Controls exist to hold a hand while someone learns to walk, not to read the diary forever.
2. **Nobody gets scared into compliance.** Fear makes kids hide things; warmth makes them come to you when something goes wrong. Coming to you is the actual goal.

## Accounts for kids: three principles

When a child gets their first email, app store account, or game login:

- **Principle 1: child accounts, not shrunken adult accounts.** Every big platform (Apple, Google, game consoles, the major app stores) offers a "child" or "family" account type. It exists for a reason: it connects the child's account to a parent's, with age-appropriate defaults and an approval flow for purchases and apps. That connection is the whole feature — it takes about ten minutes to set up, and once done, the app store does the boring enforcement for you.
- **Principle 2: their data is their story.** A kid's real name, birthdate, school, and photos form a permanent record they did not choose to publish. When in doubt: first name and a nickname are plenty for play and school accounts; the full identity keeps to official channels. This is one of those rare areas where being slightly conservative early costs nothing and buys a lot.
- **Principle 3: they learn privacy from watching you.** Saying "I never paste my password anywhere, watch" at the kitchen table teaches more than any lesson. Same with narrating your own scam-spotting. Kids copy what households do; that is genuinely good news — your habits from chapter 03 are transferable parenting.

## Shared devices: the always-wins setup

If a tablet or computer is communal, a ten-minute setup prevents 95% of household privacy collisions:

1. **Everyone gets their own account or profile.** On computers and phones this also means separate browser profiles — so your work email autofill never appears next to a kid's game login.
2. **The "main" adult account (admin) exists for changes.** Kids get standard accounts; installs and account changes come through a grown-up: the household equivalent of actually being asked at the door.
3. **Private things stay off shared devices**, or in a private browser window there (chapter 05 covers what that does and doesn't do). Shared device, shared window: that is the deal.
4. **Sign-out habits beat password habits here.** On a shared device, logging out after email or banking is the whole security model; the person borrowing the device after you is not a threat, but a logged-in session surely is something.

## Teens: power with the training wheels

Teenagers need real accounts — and a real conversation rather than a surveillance state:

- **Password manager from day one.** Their first email plus a password manager is a better graduation gift than most things money can buy; it makes "unique long password per site" the *default* they never think about. Handing over a password manager is a warm, non-preachy way to hand over responsibility.
- **Talk about screenshots, not just privacy.** A message sent is a message that can be forwarded — encryption in chapter 01 stops companies, not recipients. This is not a threat to recite daily; it is the "look before crossing" of the digital street, worth one calm conversation and a refresh at a safe moment.
- **Group chats deserve a mention.** Group messages leak more than any setting can fix: forwarding, screenshots, accidental additions. The habit worth building: nothing in a group chat you would not be fine seeing on the school notice board. That frame is teachable, not fear-mongering.
- **Location sharing: agree on a version.** Family location sharing is genuinely useful (pickup coordination, safety) *and* should be a deal everyone understands; make it explicit: with whom, always or bedtime-only, and how to pause it. Renegotiate annually — "as I grow, this shrinks" is a contract kids respect far more than a locked toggle.

## Grandparents and scam-prone relatives

Scams are, statistically, a far bigger risk to older relatives than anything else in this handbook — and the fix is happy news: **one trusting relationship beats ten settings.**

- **Make "call me first" the household vow.** The single rule that saves the most money in the real world: any call, text, or email asking for money, codes, or gift cards gets referred to one named family member before acting. Grandchildren-jail, bank-officer, IRS-impersonator, parcel-fee — the script differs, the referral rule catches them all.
- **Turn on bank and phone alerts.** A text on any card charge over a small amount is a tripwire set at the door, and it needs zero daily effort.
- **Offer, don't overhaul.** The relative's antivirus-and-VPN tower of gadgets purchased through fear is deployed guilt you are trying to remove, not add. Phone lenses can be replaced; trust cannot. Install essential things (password manager, bank alerts) and leave them feeling, "the family handled it and I understand it," not "I understand nothing."

## The household emergency folder: the cozy extra

One folder, physical or secure-digital (a password manager's shared vault is ideal), holds:

- Recovery codes for the family's important accounts (chapter 03).
- A list of accounts: email, phone plan, bank, insurance — just names and "how to reach support," never passwords in plaintext.
- The phone number of one trusted local person to call first.

This folder matters on the worst days — phone lost, accounts locked, illness — and it is also the most loving thing in this chapter: someone handled the boring stuff so a bad day doesn't have to become a disaster.

## A note on school and club accounts

Kids get accounts issued by schools, clubs, and games. A plain-English takeaway: those accounts are managed by adults at the organization, and the kind thing you can do is keep *your* home rules (password manager, unique password) applying to them too, because school email often becomes the front door of the child's whole digital identity for a decade.

And the privacy questions about what a school collects? Ask the school. Their obligations are strict and real in most countries, and asking is not combative — it is a normal, reasonable parent request.

> **Remember:** privacy, in a family, is mostly a warm habit of talking — "what did that message ask you for?" from a parent who just asks it once a month is worth more than every parental control combined.
