# What apps and websites actually collect

*Reading time: about 9 minutes.*

Here is a sentence nobody says out loud: you use apps and websites every day, and you have never once seen the list of what they actually take in. Most of us sign up, tap "agree," and get on with our lives. That is a completely reasonable way to live. This chapter is your calm, plain-English tour of what is really collected, so the picture in your head matches reality.

The goal is not to freak you out. The goal is to make you a calm, informed user — the kind who knows what is normal, knows what deserves a second look, and knows where the settings live.

## The four things nearly everyone collects

Almost every app and website you use gathers some combination of these four ingredients:

1. **What you do there.** Messages you send, searches you type, videos you watch, buttons you tap, how long you linger on a page.
2. **Who you are connected to.** Your contacts — sometimes because you gave the app access to them, sometimes because the app can infer it from who you message and how often.
3. **Where you connect from.** Your IP address, which is like the return address stamped on every letter your device sends. It shows your general area, not your doorstep.
4. **What your device is like.** Screen size, language, battery level, operating system version — dozens of small traits that, together, can act like a fingerprint for your device.

That fourth one has a name: device fingerprinting. Imagine a mall that can't see your face, but remembers *the exact combination* of your shoes, your coat, and your walking speed. The combination itself identifies you, even if no single detail does.

## What chat apps see versus who they can tell

Chat apps get their own section because the question everyone actually has is simple: **who can read my messages?**

The key idea is end-to-end encryption, or E2EE. When an app uses E2EE, your message is scrambled on your phone and only unscrambled on your friend's phone. The app's company ships the locked box but cannot open it. When an app does *not* use E2EE, the message is readable on the company's servers — usually protected for employees and outsiders, but still readable in principle.

| Situation | What that usually means |
| --- | --- |
| App with end-to-end encryption on by default | Only you and the recipient can read message contents. |
| App with E2EE off for some chats, or off entirely | The company's servers can read the contents, even if nobody in practice does. |
| Any chat app, E2EE or not | The company can see the *pattern*: who you talk to, how often, when, from where. That outer envelope is called metadata. |
| Group chats | Everyone in the group and, without E2EE, the company too. |
| Deleted messages | "Delete" usually removes your copy; copies on servers and on other people's devices may linger. |

Two small, honest disclaimers: apps change their behavior over time, and "encrypted" never beats "the person you sent it to." If you would not want a screenshot of it circulating, do not send it — encryption stops companies and strangers, not the recipient.

## What websites see

When you land on a website, the site learns: your IP address and rough location, your browser and device details, which pages you visit and in what order, and often far more through little helper files called cookies and trackers.

- **A cookie** is a small note the website leaves on your device: "this is Jane, cart has 2 items, visited Tuesday." Notes written by the site itself are convenience. Notes written by *other companies* embedded in the page — ad networks, analytics firms — are how your browsing follows you across unrelated sites.
- **Trackers** are scripts from those third parties. The share buttons, the embedded videos, the "chat with us" bubble: each is a chance for another company to be told you stopped by.
- **Login walls and newsletter popups** trade a little data for a little access. That can be a fair trade — the point is to make it *knowingly*.

If a site has more little buttons and embedded widgets than content, more parties are being told about your visit than you might guess. You do not need to live in fear of this. You just want to know it is happening.

## Why "free" is never quite free

The most useful sentence in this chapter: **when you don't pay, the product is usually your attention.** Apps and sites mostly earn money by keeping you engaged and showing you ads or upgrades. To do that well, they want two things: to know you better, and to keep you a little longer.

Both motives are, on their own, normal and even useful — recommendations that actually recommend, autofill that actually fills. The privacy conversation is about the *reach* of that knowledge: how much is collected, how long it is kept, and who else it is shared with. You are allowed to accept that trade. You are also allowed to shrink it. Both are respectable answers.

## The comfort part: what this actually means for you

Here is the part most privacy articles skip: for ordinary everyday life, the realistic risks are small and specific, not vague and cosmic. The genuinely practical worries are:

- **Scams**, which succeed through *how* a message makes you feel, not through anything collected about you (chapter 04).
- **Account takeovers**, which succeed through weak or reused passwords, not through cookies (chapter 03).
- **Embarrassing oversharing**, which happens when confidential details get pasted into the wrong window (chapter 02).
- **Leaks at companies you trusted**, which are not your fault and have a calm, five-step response (chapter 07).

Notice that none of these are "a website knows what shoe size you are." Data collection mostly feeds advertising and recommendations — annoying, worth shrinking, rarely dangerous on its own. Knowing that lets you spend your privacy energy where it pays.

## Three small moves, if you want them

1. **Give contacts, location, and microphone deliberately.** When an app asks for access, a three-second pause — "does a to-do list really need my contacts?" — is the whole skill. Saying "not now" almost never breaks an app.
2. **Prefer apps with encryption on by default for private conversations**, and know that metadata travels even so. For most people that is a comfortable enough middle.
3. **Decline the optional extras.** App consent screens do have real choices buried in them: optional data collection, personalization toggles, "help improve the product" boxes. Unchecking two boxes per app per year adds up.

> **Remember:** the goal is not zero data collection. The goal is *deliberate* data collection — you knowing roughly what leaves your device, instead of finding out from a headline.
